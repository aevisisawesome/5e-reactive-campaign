What Is Actually Going On

Everything below this line is DM-only, and it is hidden until you ask for it. It contains the answer the whole campaign is built around. A player who reads it loses three sessions of the best thing in this game.

DM-only content is showing. The red button at the bottom of the screen hides it again in one click.

There is no conspiracy, no mastermind and no secret villain. There is a two-thousand-year-old machine that has been quietly failing, a village that built three houses onto it without noticing, and a chain of accidents that has brought all of it to a head in the same tenday.

The Marrowward

It is a failing ward line, not a vault. Ancient Illefarn held this country and left works in it, and the Marrowward was a frontier ward-line — not a fortress, not a treasury, and emphatically not a tomb. It held a perimeter against incursion from the north-west, and it did that with three things working together:

  1. A ward line, anchored on six standing stones.
  2. Two gate-works at the line's ends, with living galleries, workshops and stores between them.
  3. A sentinel, which walked the line and turned back what crossed it.

It was a border post with a very long fence and one very patient guard. WP06 §3.1

The six anchors form an arc, and the arc opens to the north-west — that is the direction the line faces, and therefore the direction the threat came from. Two doors sit at its focus and its end: the quarry aperture above Greybank, and the Old Delving four watches north.

The fact the whole campaign rests on

Three of the six anchors stand inside Greybank — the tower's base course, the shrine's foundation, and the north wall of the burial ground. The village has built a tower, a shrine and a wall onto them. Ninety years of a village leaning on a fence it cannot see, and nobody has ever remarked on it.

Nothing went catastrophically wrong

There was no disaster. There was attrition. The line has been degrading for an age: anchors cracked, spans lapsed, and nobody replaced them. Three of the six spans are dead, and the dead ones are the north-west quarter — the quarter that mattered. It has been open for longer than Greybank has existed.

Things came through. Most of them died or dispersed. One got into the water galleries and stayed. And the Sentinel kept working: its instruction set survived, and its map did not.

The site is not waking up. It never stopped. Instability 1 — Dormant Disturbance — is what an ancient machine looks like when it has been quietly failing for two thousand years and nobody has poked it. Everything the party does from here is poking it. WP06 §3.3

Nothing there advances on a timer

This is worth internalising, because it is the opposite of how most dungeons behave. The site sat at Instability 1 for two thousand years and will sit there for four more sessions if nobody touches it. Every step up the clock has a name and a person who took it. If the party never opens the aperture, the site is still at 1 at the end of session four — and that is a correct outcome, not a missed one. WP06 §12, WP04 §12.2

The Sentinel

The Marrowward's defensive construct. Stone, and something else. It is not intelligent, not evil, and not negotiable — it is a machine executing an instruction set that survived while its map did not.

Its instruction, intactTurn back what crosses the line.
Its map, corruptedIt no longer knows where the line runs, and it no longer holds a current list of who is authorised to be inside it

Consequently, anything inside the perimeter that is not on its list is an incursion. The Red-Tusk were inside the perimeter. So is Greybank. So is the party, from the moment they step past the boards.

The goblins call it the Counter, because it walks the same path and takes the same number of steps, every time.

Why it cannot be killed

At levels 3 to 6 it cannot be meaningfully damaged. Damage from a single source of less than 25 leaves no mark at all. There is no hit point total and no bloodied threshold — a party cannot damage it into stopping. A party that commits to a stand-up fight in the Long Gallery should die there, and the campaign will have told them four times before they do.

There are three ways to hold a name, and none of them runs through a fight: Orbal recites it from the cords, the site's own records give it up after a full watch of patient reading, or Nethra tells you. Every route to the deepest part of this dungeon runs through a faction relationship, an hour of reading, or a climb down a broken fence on an open moor. WP06 §5.3

What it is worth alive

It is the reason the deep galleries are not full of moor animals. A party that removes it and then congratulates itself should be given, next session, the sight of the Moorgaunt in the Long Gallery — walking a route it has never dared take before.

The Red-Tusk, and the door they are holding shut

Sixty-one goblins: 22 who can fight, 39 who cannot, 11 wounded from whatever displaced them. They lived at the Old Delving until something came out of the deep place at night, killed methodically, and could not be fought. They left, they piled the doorway shut from the outside with rock and timber, and they did not stay to watch.

They have maintained that pile for a generation, out of terror, without understanding what they were containing. Sixty-one starving refugees at Kettlerock have been, entirely by accident, the only thing keeping the north gate of the Marrowward shut.

The consequence nobody sees coming

If the Red-Tusk leave — the Flight branch — nobody re-stacks the pile. Weather and the slope do the rest, the north gate becomes permanently breached, and the Sentinel walks out. Its circuit runs the length of the line, and the line runs to Greybank.

On night three the encounter is not a fight. It is an evacuation, a corridor, and a choice about which building the party stands in front of. Defense does not help. The militia's billhooks do nothing. Berrick Hone will try anyway.

Why this is fair, and not a trap

Nothing about it is retroactive. Ghesh and Orbal both say what they ran from, from session two, and both accounts are accurate as far as they go. The pile is visibly maintained — any party that reaches the Old Delving sees rock stacked by hands, recently. The Watch-Glass shows the line, and the line ends at Greybank. And the party reaches the Flight branch by fighting refugees instead of asking what they were running from. Reversing it: re-stack the pile, shut the gate from inside, or solve the Sentinel properly.

The Moorgaunt, and why the moor is empty

A long-limbed quadruped the colour of wet limestone, built for scree and cold, and visibly starving. Its ribs are the memorable thing: a party that sees it in daylight describes it afterwards as thin, not as huge.

It is not ancient, not magical, and not connected to the site. It is an animal that ran out of country.

Sixty-one goblins have eaten the moor's game to nothing across two seasons. The Moorgaunt's range collapsed with it, so it came south and west looking for food, found the failed spans at the top of the Broken Quarter, and came down the hole because cold air and the smell of old bone came up it. It has been working inward and outward ever since.

One shortage causes two problems. The stripped moor moves the Red-Tusk clock and puts a predator on Greybank's doorstep, and neither is anyone's plan. There is no mastermind here and no coincidence — just a shortage with two consequences. WP07 §9.2

Three names for one animal

The Red-Tusk call it the Thin Dog — they have watched it for two seasons and they avoid it. Bessa Ruddock, who has seen the drag marks and not the animal, calls it “the lean thing”. And the site's own records do not mention it at all, which is itself information: this is not part of the Marrowward.

Read that table carefully. It says four things.

  1. The Red-Tusk drive the campaign if nobody else does. Theirs is the only clock that advances on its own biology.
  2. The Laughing Hollow does not move at all. Their thread is entirely player-driven, and a party that never touches the site never meets an elf. That is correct.
  3. Illefarn stays at 1. The site does not wake by itself. Somebody has to open it — the party, or the quarry expansion, and the expansion needs the party to fund it or Daggerford to.
  4. Greybank ends session four at Reaction 5 with Defense 2 and a warband forming.

The default outcome of this campaign is the village losing, slowly and legibly, over four sessions in which it warned everyone. This is the baseline the party is being invited to change — and if they do not change it, that is a result, not a failure of the game.

Part 3

Starting the Campaign

How the party arrives, what is open to them, and roughly where four sessions can go. Nothing in this part is a spoiler — it is the shape of the campaign, not its content.

The four arrival hooks

None of these is a patron and none of them gives the party an instruction. That is the whole design: the campaign has no quest-giver, and a hook that tells the party what to do would undo Part 0's first page. WP08 §4.1

#HookWhat it givesWhat it does not give
1 Passing through, southbound. Greybank is the only bridge for a day's travel; the party is crossing and needs a bed Total freedom, and a reason to already be at the bridge and the toll-shed Nothing. The party may leave, and the campaign lets them
2 Hired by Sember Dray, by letter, to find out why the cart is late A reason to talk to Gedd Plank in the first hour, and 15 gp Any authority. Sember cannot pay more and will say so
3 One PC is from here — the empty cott H22 is theirs, or their family's Free village knowledge, an existing attitude, and a household Popularity. It puts them in a specific cott with specific neighbours
4 Following the stone. Somebody in Daggerford mentioned that Greybank's pale limestone is cut wrong A reason to go to the quarry on Day 1, and a connection to Serina Malk that costs nothing Any idea what that means
The recommendation, and why

Hook 1 or hook 3. Both put the party in Greybank with no obligations at all, which is the state the campaign is designed to be played from — the first session is about a village becoming interesting, and an errand competes with that.

Hook 2 is the safety option for a group that wants to be told why they are somewhere. Its ceiling is deliberately low: 15 gp against a village that holds 260, so it cannot become the campaign.

Hook 4 is the most dangerous and the most interesting — it points the party at the quarry on Day 1, before the collapse, which changes how session one plays. Use it if your group likes pulling at threads.

One rule

Do not combine a hook with a deadline of its own. If the party has somewhere else to be, session one has failed before it starts.

The three doors

Session one puts three problems in front of the party at once. None is mandatory, and each is a door into a different half of the campaign rather than a quest with a reward at the end.

Door 1

The fold

Four sheep gone in eleven days, a wall with two different hands on it, and a shepherd who reports rather than asks.

Opens: the Red-Tusk thread — sixty-one refugees deciding whether the humans are a door or a wall.

Costs to look: a quarter of a watch, in daylight, with no check at all.

Door 2

The Pale

Six quiet men, a foreman who checks boards he told everyone to leave alone, and a smith who says no chisel makes that cut.

Opens: the site — and therefore the elves, and the ending.

Costs to look: one watch underground, plus a quarter each way.

Door 3

The far bank

Charcoal four days late, and a man alone across the water who has felt watched for years and is not believed.

Opens: the Laughing Hollow — their route to the village runs through here, entirely under cover.

Costs to look: a watch, and the skiff seats four.

And Daggerford, which arrives as an offer

The fourth faction does not present a problem. It presents money. Daggerford is two days away and always two days away, and it never sends armed help for free — its hired guards protect cargo, and only cargo.

What it sends instead is Serina Malk, alone, on night one, buying drinks for the quarry crew, and later Corben Threll with a contract. She is the campaign's most dangerous NPC and she never threatens anybody: she simply offers the party more money than the village contains, for the one thing they have not yet been told not to sell.

The Daggerford clock moves on information, not on violence. It advances when an artifact or a true account of one reaches Daggerford — which means the party moves it by talking. If they ignore Malk, she keeps buying drinks and the clock stays at 1.

Where it can go

Four sessions, with an optional fifth if the group explores slowly. Shape, not plot — the ending emerges from what the players do rather than from a canonical finale.

Session zero notes

Ten minutes before anyone rolls anything. Say these four things out loud, because the fairness contract only works if both sides know it exists.

1 Death is expected

Character death is an accepted outcome, not a failure. The world does not scale to the party, and some things in it cannot be beaten by fighting. What the campaign promises in exchange is that danger will be readable before it is fatal — three signals before the fourth, every time.

2 Backups are encouraged

Ask each player to have at least one backup character sketched. A replacement enters at the party's current level, needs minimal narrative justification, is not penalised for arriving late, and is introduced quickly.

What death costs is not power. It costs relationships, knowledge, equipment, reputation and personal objectives. It must never cost the player a permanently weaker character.

3 Retreat is legitimate

Players are explicitly expected to scout, retreat, negotiate, prepare and exploit terrain. Say that walking away from a fight is a valid move and will never be punished as cowardice. Most enemies in this campaign would rather live too — opening an escape route for them is a tactic, not a mercy.

4 Consequences persist, and there is no villain

The world does not reset between sessions. What is spent is gone, what is broken stays broken, and the dead stay dead. And nobody is orchestrating any of it — there are four groups of people with incompatible needs and no time, and the pressure comes from that.

One thing not to say

Do not tell them the default outcome. Part 2 is DM-only for a reason: a table that knows the village loses by default plays to beat a puzzle rather than to help a place. Tell them the world is harsh and readable. Let them find out that it is also losing.
