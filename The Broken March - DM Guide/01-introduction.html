Part 0

How to Run This Campaign

Read this once. It is the part that makes everything after it make sense, and it is short on purpose. If you take nothing else from it, take the last section.

What kind of campaign this is

The Broken March is four sessions of D&D 5e for characters starting at level 3 and ending somewhere around 5 or 6. It takes place in and around one village, Greybank, on the north bank of a river called the Marrow, two days from Daggerford. The region is small — eighteen places, and you could walk to the far edge of it in a day and a half. That smallness is the whole design.

It is mechanics-first and story-light. There is no plot waiting to be uncovered and no arc the players are supposed to follow. What there is instead is a set of situations that are already in motion, a village whose condition is written down as numbers, and a rule that nearly every meaningful thing the players do changes what happens next. The story is what that produces.

The intended feeling is one the design document names directly:

“D&D Dark Souls” through consequences, hostile systems, dangerous unknowns and problem-solving — not simply inflated monster statistics. Design doc §1

Players are expected to scout, retreat, negotiate, prepare and exploit terrain. A party that treats every problem as a fight will lose characters, and the campaign is built so that losing them is legible rather than unlucky.

Three chips appear throughout this guide, marking where material comes from. Lore is established Forgotten Realms canon. Adapted is Realms canon bent to fit this campaign. Campaign is invented for The Broken March and exists nowhere else. You do not need to care about the distinction to run the game; it is there so that if a player asks “is that a real place?”, you can answer.

The five rules that make it different

None of these is a house rule you have to remember at the table. They are decisions already baked into every document behind this guide. They are here so that when the campaign does something that feels harsh, you know it was meant to.

1 The world does not scale

Encounter presence is determined by the world, not by what the party can handle. A level 3 party can meet creatures it can beat, creatures it can beat only with preparation, creatures it can survive but not beat, and creatures that cannot be beaten conventionally at all. Nothing adjusts itself downward because the party arrived early.

Worked: the site under the quarry has four stages, and stage three holds something that a level 3 party cannot damage. Walking four watches north to the other entrance drops them straight into it. The campaign will not stop them, and it will not soften what is there. Design doc §2.1, WP06 §4.1

2 Combat is one solution among many — and some things cannot be killed

Difficult encounters are built with environmental weaknesses, behavioural patterns, terrain, faction relationships and escape routes. The intended player question is often not how do we kill this but how do we solve this.

Worked: the thing that patrols the deep galleries ignores damage under 25 from any single source and has no hit point total at all. It also will not step outside the ward line — and an object the party can find at level 3, for free, shows exactly where that line runs. The answer is a map, not a sword. WP07 §11, WP06 §6.3

3 Consequences persist

The world does not reset between sessions. Resources spent are gone, buildings destroyed stay destroyed, and the dead stay dead — their skills, relationships and services permanently lost unless somebody plausibly replaces them, which mostly nobody does.

Worked: Greybank has one wright. Without her, every building and repair project in the campaign takes 50% longer, permanently. There is no competent joiner on the quarry crew, there will not be one, and a party that never spoke to her will find out from the numbers. Design doc §2.3, WP03 §5.7

4 There is no villain

No hidden mastermind controls the factions. Each of the four genuinely pursues its own interests, each is internally divided, and none of them is evil. The pressure in this campaign comes from four groups of people with incompatible needs and no time.

Worked: sixty-one goblins ate the moor's game to nothing over two seasons, because they had to. That one shortage does two things — it starves them into a decision, and it drives a hungry predator south onto the village's flock. Nobody planned either. Design doc §7, WP07 §9.2

5 Prepare what happens if the players do nothing

This is the operating principle the whole state model exists to serve. You do not prepare a route. You prepare the world's own momentum, and then let the players alter or replace it. Everything downstream — clocks, decay tables, threat stages — is machinery for answering what happens anyway.

Worked: there is a written table of what all five clocks do across four sessions with zero player involvement. You will find it in Part 2. Start every session by asking which of those columns the players moved. Design doc §25, WP04 §12.2

The fairness contract

The campaign is allowed to be harsh. It is not allowed to be deceptive. The distinction is carried entirely by telegraphing, and it works in four levels that escalate toward the player.

The four threat-signalling levels Design doc §6, WP07 §3.3
LevelMeansMust beExample from this campaign
1 — Suspicion An environmental cue Present in a place the party crosses anyway Something has been using the sunken lane that is neither a warden nor a villager
2 — Warning Evidence of danger Physical, and readable without magic Four ewes in eleven days, a drag trail running uphill, and a tally on a slate
3 — Explicit warning Direct evidence that fighting this is extremely dangerous From a named NPC, or an unmissable object Eleven bodies on the dry steps above a flooded stair, and not one in the water
4 — Immediate threat They were told, and continued — The thing is in the room

Once level 4 is reached, you are not required to protect anyone. That is the exchange. In return for the campaign signalling extreme danger three times, allowing clever plans to work, and never retroactively changing facts to defeat a player's plan, it asks you not to soften the fourth. Design doc §29

The obligation this creates is telegraphing, not mitigation. Where something in this campaign is unrecoverable, it is signalled three times and then allowed to happen. Players are told at session zero that death is expected and that backup characters are a good idea.

The vocabulary

Every campaign-specific term, in one line each. You do not need to memorise this. Every one of these words links to this table from anywhere else in the guide, so you can check a definition without losing your place.

Glossary WP01 §22
TermMeans
watch Four hours. Six to a day: Dawn, Morning, Afternoon, Evening, Night, Deep Night. A short rest is a quarter of one; a long rest is two. World events fire on their own stated deadlines, not because the party rested.
Food Village variable, 0–6, starts at 4. Whether Greybank eats. A major livestock attack costs 1.
Trade Village variable, 0–6, starts at 3. Whether goods reach the village. At 1 or below, imports stop entirely.
Defense Village variable, 0–6, starts at 2. What response the village can mount. At 2 that means eleven militia, in about twenty minutes.
Unity Village variable, 0–6, starts at 4. Whether the village can still decide things together. A village that cannot agree cannot be organised into anything.
reputation How a whole faction regards the party, −3 to +3, tracked separately for each of the four. It changes access, not inventory.
attitude What one individual thinks — Hostile, Wary, Neutral, Friendly. Tracked separately from reputation and able to contradict it: the miller's daughter stays Friendly at Greybank −2 because the party saved her family.
Greybank Reaction Clock, 0–6, starts at 1. How the village is responding: 1 Routine Governance · 2 Concern · 3 Organized Response · 4 Emergency Measures · 5 Mobilization · 6 Political Outcome.
Hollow Intervention Clock, 0–6, starts at 1. How close the elves are to acting: 1 Observation · 2 Contact · 3 Warning · 4 Interference · 5 Containment · 6 Settlement.
Red-Tusk Strategic Clock, 0–6, starts at 1, and the only one that branches. Stages 1–3 are common — Displaced Scouting, Resource Pressure, Strategic Decision — then it splits into War, Compact or Flight.
Daggerford Commercial Clock, 0–6, starts at 1. How interested the money is: 1 Rumor · 2 Inquiry · 3 Opportunity · 4 Investment · 5 Leverage · 6 Settlement. It moves on information, not violence.
Illefarn Instability Clock, 0–6, starts at 1. How awake the site under the quarry is: 0 Quiescent to 6 Catastrophic Transformation. It sat at 1 for two thousand years. Nothing there advances on a timer.
threat ladder The six rungs every unresolved problem climbs: 1 Sign · 2 Incident · 3 Escalation · 4 Response · 5 Collateral consequence · 6 Regional outcome. Threats advance only on defined triggers.
telegraphing The four signalling levels above — Suspicion, Warning, Explicit warning, Immediate threat. The campaign's promise that harsh is not arbitrary.
infrastructure Buildings with gameplay effects, each in one state: Improved · Intact · Damaged · Disabled · Destroyed. The rule that catches DMs out: an intact building without its person provides nothing.
sector state The site's sixteen sectors, each: Unknown · Discovered · Accessible · Locked · Breached · Sealed. All start Unknown. Two can apply at once — Discovered / Locked.
system state The site's seven mechanisms, each: Unknown · Identified · Inactive · Active · Damaged · Disabled. Activating one must define what notices.
breach A sealed area opened, a construct released, a ward perimeter broken. A fact about the world, not a number — and the one thing in the site that does not undo itself when Instability later falls.
investigation What a serious crime opens: discovery → witnesses → evidence → suspects → surveillance → arrest. It runs on its own schedule whether or not the party engages. No courtroom subsystem.
evidence Weak, Moderate or Strong. Testimony has quality, not just existence — the same act at Deep Night and at a working watch produces different evidence.
critical stock The short list you actually count: potions, healer's kits, antitoxin, the chain shirt, the shield, the horses, the component packages, the blasting powder. Spent is gone. Refilling needs a living provider, enough Trade, and elapsed time.
Loads Stone Loads and Timber Loads — the unit every building project is priced in, alongside coin and days.
labour levy The Reeve's power to call volunteers — twenty at Unity 4. Every village project needs it, which is why every village project needs her.
blocs Greybank does not vote as one thing: Leadership, Craft, Trade, Quarry, Farm, Militia. Nine of the eighteen militia are quarry crew, so the village cannot mobilise and quarry at the same time.
boundary stones The edge of what Greybank will defend. The militia will never fight beyond them — not to rescue, not to pursue, not for the party.
morale 1d6 on a trigger: 1 rout · 2 break · 3–4 waver · 5 hold · 6 harden. Triggers include an escape route being opened, which makes giving the enemy a way out a tactic.
cascade limit Automatic consequence chains stop after two links, unless one major event genuinely changed several systems at once. This exists so a burned building does not cost you an evening of accounting.

The honest warning

You should know before session one how this campaign goes if the players never engage with it, because it is written down and because it is not a failure state.

The Red-Tusk clock is the only one that advances on its own — their food is failing, which is biology rather than intent. So: their food fails in session two and an envoy comes. Nobody answers it, so raiding begins in session three. By session four the road is watched and the village is isolating. Meanwhile Greybank climbs from Concern to Mobilization, having warned everyone at every step, and arrives at the end of session four at Defense 2, with Food 2, farms partly abandoned, and a warband forming that it cannot repel.

The elves never move. Nobody meets one. The site stays asleep, because nothing there wakes up by itself and somebody has to open it.

The default outcome of this campaign is Greybank losing, slowly and legibly, over four sessions in which it warned everybody. That is the baseline the players are being invited to change — and if they do not change it, do not steer them away from it. A campaign in which the village loses because nobody helped is this design working, not failing. WP04 §12.2

Part 1

The Setting

One village, eighteen places around it, and four groups of people who want incompatible things. This is everything you need to picture before session one; the reference pages have the detail.

Greybank

Greybank is a stone village that ran out of reasons to be one. A hundred and forty-three people live on the north bank of the Marrow at the only bridge for a day's walk in either direction, cutting pale limestone out of a hillside their great-grandparents did not dig and cannot account for. The stone goes to Daggerford. The coin comes back thin. The quarry face has begun opening onto worked walls nobody built, the militia is eleven farmers with a shed of billhooks, and the earth ring that used to enclose the village has been breached in four places for thirty years because nothing has needed to come through it. Something has now started needing to. WP02 §3.1

Beyond the pitch, five facts do most of the work. Each of them is a constraint the players will run into, and each is deliberate.

The five facts that shape everything WP02 §22
FactWhy it is there
≈260 gp of liquid coin exists in the entire village Greybank cannot buy the party's loot, and robbing it is not lucrative — taking the coin destroys every service the party depends on. Selling anything of value means Daggerford, which advances a clock. WP02 §10.5
Seven ways in, and the village watches two The bridge, the haul road, the north footpath, the east fields, the south-east riverside path, the shoal at low water, and the wharf by boat. Only the first two are watched, and the militia captain has raised this twice. WP02 §4.7
The ring is breached in four places North-west, north, east and south-east, in gaps 20 to 40 feet wide and completely open. It was breached deliberately, to widen the haul road, and nobody meant it to stay that way. This is why Defense is 2. WP02 §7 G21
The bell does not reach five of the places people work Not the quarry, the bunkhouse, the fold, the charcoal camp or the outlying farmsteads — where 40 of the 143 are during the working day. Session one is built to teach the players this. WP02 §12.4
Defense 2 means eleven militia, in about twenty minutes Eighteen names on the roll, eleven turn out promptly, and nine of the eighteen are quarry crew. The fold is 260 yards away and unlit: at Defense 2 it burns before anyone arrives. That is not a flaw. It is the reason the players will be asked to care. WP02 §12.2–12.3
Worth saying out loud

Everything inside the earth ring is under 30 rounds from everything else at a run. The quarry is 70 rounds — about seven minutes — and that gap is deliberate. Help from the quarry never arrives during a fight in the village. WP02 §4.5

The region

Eighteen places, and distances measured in watches on foot. The whole map is walkable: the furthest thing on it is five watches away, and the party will spend most of the campaign inside the first two. Daggerford is two days to the south-east — and it is always two days. Nothing in this campaign gets help from Daggerford quickly.

Travel is measured in watches, not miles, and a watch spent walking is a watch not spent doing anything else. That is the whole time-pressure system: the party has six blocks a day, a long rest costs two of them, and problems have deadlines that do not wait.

The four factions

Four groups, none of them evil, none of them united. The internal split is the most important line in each panel below.

A faction played as a monolith is played wrong. Each of these has two people inside it who want different things, and the party can reach both.

Greybank

Reaction clock 1 — Routine Governance · Party reputation 0

A village that would like everyone to go away and let it work. It acts through the moot, the labour levy, the militia, and the refusal of service. Its strength is a hundred and forty-three people who know the ground.

Wants
To reach harvest, then winter, intact.
The internal split
Five blocs, and two men counting the same eighteen bodies. The militia captain wants the ring closed and the tower manned; the quarry foreman wants his nine militia-roll men off the roll so he can keep cutting. Nine of the eighteen are quarry crew, so the village cannot mobilise and quarry at the same time — and both men are right.
Red line
Harm to its children.
Will never
Fight beyond the boundary stones. Not to rescue, not to pursue.
Wants from the party
That they be useful, and then leave.

WP04 §3, §4

Laughing Hollow

Intervention clock 1 — Observation · Party reputation 0

A small number of people who are right, and who have decided that explaining why is not worth the risk. A warden-band of nine, of whom the party will ever see three. Nobody in Greybank has seen an elf in a generation.

Wants
The site under the quarry left closed.
The internal split
Vaerelin Ossuine wants containment through restriction; Nethra wants containment through alliance. He has authority. She has watched the village for eleven years and has been right before. At stages 3–4 that argument is live, and a party that reaches Nethra gets a different faction than one that only ever meets Vaerelin.
Red line
The site being opened wider.
Will never
Explain themselves fully. They watch first and speak once.
Wants from the party
That they stop.

WP04 §3, §5

The Red-Tusk

Strategic clock 1 — Displaced Scouting · Party reputation −1

Refugees with weapons, deciding this tenday whether the humans are a door or a wall. Sixty-one goblins: 22 who can fight, 39 who cannot, 11 wounded from whatever displaced them. Not a warband — a population with weapons, and every decision they make is constrained by thirty-nine people who cannot travel fast.

Wants
Somewhere to be that is not where they were.
The internal split
Ghesh Two-Knife has buried too many and believes the humans will never share; Orbal the Rememberer believes there was a bargain here once and could be again. Neither commands — the band follows whoever was right most recently — and the clock's stage-3 branch is that argument being settled, into War, Compact or Flight.
Red line
Being made to go back.
Will never
Return to where they came from.
Wants from the party
To know which they are — enemy, partner, or obstacle.
Read the −1 correctly

They start at −1 because the party looks like the sort of people who wrong them. They have not been wronged by this party. The whole faction is reachable from there.

WP04 §3, §6

Daggerford Interests

Commercial clock 1 — Rumor · Party reputation 0

Two men who both want something from Greybank and who want different things. Their method is money, contracts and patience, and their weakness is that they cannot make anyone do anything.

Wants
Stone, and whatever the ruins hold.
The internal split
Corben Threll wants a supply contract and a commercial post, and would rather the odd business at the quarry stayed a rumour — an investigation is a delay. Serina Malk wants the artifacts, authenticated and exclusive, and an investigation is exactly what she is running. They are not allies; they are two claims on the same village.
Red line
Losing the stone contract.
Will never
Send armed help for free. Hired guards protect cargo, and only cargo.
Wants from the party
An inventory.

The village cast

Sixteen people session one actually uses, out of a roster of thirty-four. Five of them must land — if the session ends and the players cannot describe those five, the session did not work. Five others are marked load-bearing: those are the people whose death removes a service the village cannot replace, and you should know which they are before anyone swings at anything.

Who not to introduce in session one

No elves — the Hollow is at Observation and nobody in Greybank has seen one in a generation. Not Ghesh or Orbal — the Red-Tusk are scouts at a distance, and the envoy is a session two event. Not Corben Threll, who is two days away and does not travel on a rumour. And especially not Nethra: she is the most valuable NPC the party has to earn, and meeting her early costs the campaign its best reveal. Serina Malk is the one exception, and she should be there.
