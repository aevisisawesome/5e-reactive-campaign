## 1. Campaign Summary

**Working title:** The Broken March  
**System:** Dungeons & Dragons 5e  
**Format:** In-person campaign  
**Primary length:** 4 sessions  
**Contingency length:** Optional 5th session if progress is slower than expected  
**Starting level:** 3  
**Expected ending level:** 5–6  
**Primary location:** Greybank, a small village and its surrounding wilderness near Daggerford  
**Setting:** Forgotten Realms / Faerûn  
**Campaign style:** Reactive sandbox, tactical problem-solving, persistent consequences, lethal exploration, emergent narrative  
**Story emphasis:** Low-to-medium  
**Gameplay/mechanics emphasis:** Very high

The campaign is designed around a small, persistent environment in which nearly every meaningful player action changes future play.

The intended feeling is:

> **“D&D Dark Souls” through consequences, hostile systems, dangerous unknowns, and problem-solving — not simply inflated monster statistics.**

Players are explicitly expected to:
- scout;
- retreat;
- negotiate;
- prepare;
- exploit terrain;
- manipulate factions;
- gather information;
- solve encounters indirectly;
- accept that some problems are initially too dangerous to fight head-on.

Characters who repeatedly face-check unknown dangers, ignore warnings, or treat the setting as consequence-free may die.

Players have been informed in advance that spare characters are recommended.

# 2. Design Pillars

## 2.1 The World Does Not Scale to the Players

The setting exists independently of character level.

A level 3 party may encounter:
- creatures they can defeat;
- creatures they can defeat only with preparation;
- creatures they can survive but probably cannot defeat;
- creatures that are effectively impossible to defeat conventionally.

Encounter presence is determined by the world, not by expected CR balance.

The DM should provide information, warning signs, environmental evidence, NPC knowledge, and opportunities for retreat.

The game should be harsh, but not arbitrary.

**Core principle:**

> Dangerous situations should usually be readable before they become fatal.

Examples:
- enormous footprints;
- partially eaten armored soldiers;
- NPC warnings;
- melted stone;
- broken weapons;
- unusual silence in the forest;
- evidence that a previous adventuring party failed;
- enemies fleeing from something worse.

If players ignore clear evidence, the campaign allows the consequences to stand.

---

## 2.2 Combat Is Only One Solution

Major encounters should rarely be designed as simple HP races.

Whenever practical, difficult encounters should contain:
- environmental weaknesses;
- behavioral patterns;
- factional relationships;
- terrain advantages;
- traps;
- exploitable resources;
- alternative objectives;
- escape routes;
- negotiation possibilities;
- ways to avoid the encounter entirely.

A monster may be conventionally overwhelming but manageable through intelligent play.

Examples:
- lure it beneath a collapsing quarry ceiling;
- poison its food;
- trap it behind an Illefarn ward;
- convince goblins to attack from another direction;
- ignite flammable gas;
- destroy what it is protecting rather than the creature itself;
- steal an object while distracting it;
- force it into daylight;
- flood its lair;
- seal the entrance.

The intended player question is not always:

> “How do we kill this?”

It should often become:

> “How do we solve this?”

---

## 2.3 Consequences Are Persistent

The world does not reset between sessions.

Resources, relationships, infrastructure, deaths, crimes, destroyed buildings, and faction attitudes remain changed.

Examples:

### Shopkeeper murdered
The shopkeeper is dead.

Unless another plausible merchant eventually moves into Greybank:
- the shop stays closed;
- its inventory does not respawn;
- basic equipment becomes difficult to acquire;
- trade may decline;
- relatives and neighbors react;
- villagers investigate.

### Bridge destroyed
The bridge remains destroyed.

Consequences may include:
- trade reduction;
- delayed reinforcements;
- food shortages;
- safer passage for monsters;
- increased prices.

### Friendly NPC killed
That NPC remains dead.

Their:
- skills;
- relationships;
- resources;
- information;
- quests;
- services

are permanently lost unless another character can realistically replace them.

# 3. Faerûn Integration

The campaign should explicitly distinguish published Forgotten Realms lore from campaign-original material.

Use the following tags in all future campaign documents:

**[LORE]** — grounded in published Forgotten Realms lore.

**[CAMPAIGN]** — invented specifically for The Broken March.

**[ADAPTED]** — based on established Forgotten Realms material but altered for campaign use.

---

## 3.1 Regional Placement

**[LORE]** Daggerford is a fortified settlement on the Sword Coast south of Waterdeep and has historically served as a frontier base for adventures into nearby wilderness.

**[LORE]** The region contains remnants associated with ancient Illefarn and later elven/dwarven activity.

**[LORE]** The High Moor and surrounding wilderness contain goblinoids, trolls, monsters, ruins, and isolated settlements.

**[LORE]** Laughing Hollow and nearby forested regions support elven and fey presence.

These elements justify the campaign's:
- ancient ruins;
- isolated settlement;
- dangerous wilderness;
- goblinoid factions;
- elven interest;
- frontier economy.

---

## 3.2 Greybank

**[CAMPAIGN]**

Greybank is a small village roughly two days from Daggerford.

Approximate population:

**120–160 inhabitants**

The entire campaign remains geographically centered on:
- Greybank;
- surrounding farms;
- river crossing;
- quarry;
- nearby woodland;
- nearby ruins;
- goblin territory;
- several wilderness locations.

The small scope is deliberate.

The goal is for players to develop familiarity with the physical environment and population.

By Session 3, players should recognize locations and NPCs without needing exposition.

# 4. Campaign Structure

The campaign is built for four sessions with an optional fifth.

The structure should support variable player pacing without forcing artificial progress.

## Session Framework

### Session 1
**Establish the village and destabilize the region.**

Primary functions:
- introduce Greybank;
- establish important NPCs;
- establish resource scarcity;
- demonstrate that the world is dangerous;
- open the Illefarn site;
- present several simultaneous problems.

Expected character progression:

**Level 3 → Level 4 near session end**

---

### Session 2
**Consequences begin to branch.**

Primary functions:
- player-driven choice of objectives;
- faction reactions;
- first major encounter that strongly punishes brute force;
- criminal/social consequences become visible;
- ignored threats advance.

Expected level:

**Level 4**

---

### Session 3
**The region destabilizes significantly.**

Primary functions:
- previously ignored problems escalate;
- alliances or enemies become established;
- deeper Illefarn mechanisms become available;
- village state visibly reflects player behavior;
- larger tactical/environmental encounter.

Expected progression:

**Level 4 → Level 5**

---

### Session 4
**Convergence and resolution.**

The major systems collide:
- village;
- goblins;
- elves;
- Illefarn machinery;
- regional threat.

The ending should emerge from player choices rather than requiring one canonical finale.

Expected ending level:

**Level 5 or 6**

---

### Optional Session 5

Used only if:
- players explore more slowly;
- major content remains unresolved;
- Session 4 naturally becomes setup rather than climax;
- the group wants additional resolution.

Session 5 must not be mandatory for the campaign to function.

---

# 5. Character Death and Replacement

Character death is an accepted campaign outcome.

The campaign should remain playable after deaths without destroying momentum.

## Replacement Character Rules

Players are encouraged to prepare at least one backup character.

A replacement character should:
- enter at the party's current level;
- require minimal narrative justification;
- not be penalized for arriving late;
- be introduced quickly.

Possible entry sources:
- travelers;
- mercenaries;
- Daggerford reinforcements;
- wandering adventurers;
- Greybank residents;
- members of factions;
- survivors from previous expeditions.

Character death should hurt through lost:
- relationships;
- knowledge;
- equipment;
- reputation;
- personal objectives.

It should **not** punish the player with a permanently weaker replacement character.

---

# 6. Information Before Danger

The campaign must distinguish **harsh** from **arbitrary**.

Fatal threats should normally provide clues.

The party may still misunderstand or ignore those clues.

## Threat Signaling Levels

### Level 1 — Suspicion
Subtle environmental cues.

Example:
- strange tracks;
- silence;
- missing animals.

### Level 2 — Warning
Evidence of danger.

Example:
- mutilated corpses;
- destroyed carts;
- terrified witnesses.

### Level 3 — Explicit Warning
Direct evidence that conventional combat is extremely dangerous.

Example:

> “Six militia went inside. One came back.”

### Level 4 — Immediate Threat
The players have deliberately continued despite warnings.

At this point the DM is not required to protect them.

# 7. Faction Structure

The campaign contains four principal factions.

No hidden mastermind controls all factions.

Each faction genuinely pursues its own interests.

---

## 7.1 Greybank Villagers

**[CAMPAIGN]**

Primary priorities:

1. Safety
2. Food
3. Economic survival
4. Stability

Internal disagreements prevent the village from acting as a single mind.

Possible blocs:
- traditionalists;
- traders;
- militia advocates;
- quarry workers;
- farmers;
- isolationists.

Greybank should react strongly to player conduct.

---

## 7.2 Laughing Hollow Elves

**[LORE / CAMPAIGN]**

The regional elven presence is lore-compatible.

The specific representatives and their involvement with Greybank are campaign-original.

Primary priorities:
- prevent dangerous Illefarn systems from being disturbed;
- prevent artifact theft;
- contain ancient threats;
- protect nearby territory.

They are not necessarily friendly.

They may be correct about the danger while being indifferent to Greybank's economic survival.

---

## 7.3 Red-Tusk Goblins

**[CAMPAIGN]**

A local goblin group displaced from territory connected to the ruins.

Primary priorities:
- survival;
- territory;
- food;
- leverage.

They can become:
- enemies;
- trading partners;
- temporary allies;
- informants;
- rivals.

Players who automatically massacre them may eliminate potentially valuable strategic options.

---

## 7.4 Daggerford Commercial Interests

**[LORE / CAMPAIGN]**

Daggerford's regional importance is lore-based.

Specific merchants and agents are campaign-original.

Primary priorities:
- acquire artifacts;
- secure trade;
- profit from instability;
- expand influence.

They provide money and equipment but may remove valuable artifacts from the campaign permanently.

# 9. Resource Scarcity

> **✅ Implemented by WP01 — see [[WP01 — Campaign State Model]] §5 *Resource Economy*:** the Class A / B / C model, pricing and the single-worst-scarcity rule, medicine, ammunition, weapons and armour, horses, timber and stone, replenishment by Trade tier, and service-provider death.
> The starting stock in WP01 §5.2 are **WP01 defaults**; WP02 may assign them to specific buildings and owners, but may not change the class model or the replenishment rules.

Greybank does not have infinite inventory.

Track important resources.

Examples:
- healing potions;
- ammunition;
- armor;
- food;
- horses;
- spell components;
- construction materials;
- medicine.

If resources are consumed, stolen, destroyed, or lost:

**they remain unavailable until logically replenished.**

Example starting stock:

- 2 healing potions;
- 1 chain shirt;
- 1 shield;
- limited arrows and bolts;
- no plate armor;
- no magical weapons for sale.

This turns logistics into gameplay.

# 10. Crime and Law

> **✅ Implemented by WP01 — see [[WP01 — Campaign State Model]] §7 *Crime System* (severity tiers, evidence categories, the evidence rule, concealment) and §9 *Village Hostility and Mobilization*.**
> WP01 §9 confirms the promise made below: a level 3–5 party openly fighting the whole settlement faces an extremely high probability of death — and it is run as an environmental/social military situation, not a sequence of balanced fights. Escape and surrender remain possible.

Crime should generate systemic consequences.

NPCs do not forget serious crimes because the scene ended.

## Murder Example

If the players murder the shopkeeper:

1. The shopkeeper dies permanently.
2. The shop closes.
3. Witnesses react.
4. Family/friends become suspicious.
5. Village leadership investigates.
6. Evidence may implicate the party.
7. Rumors spread.
8. Reputation changes.
9. Services may be denied.
10. Arrest or violence may follow.

If the village becomes convinced that the party murdered an innocent resident, the situation becomes extremely dangerous.

A small village can field:
- militia;
- hunters;
- guards;
- barricades;
- dogs;
- archers;
- alarm bells;
- improvised defenses.

The villagers know the terrain and vastly outnumber a low-level adventuring party.

A level 3–5 party attempting to openly fight the entire village should have an **extremely high probability of death**.

The DM should not artificially convert twenty angry villagers into conveniently spaced balanced encounters.

# 11. Investigation System

> **✅ Implemented by WP01 — see [[WP01 — Campaign State Model]] §8 *Investigation Mechanics*:** advancement timing, automatic and bonus advancement, stalling, reversal, murder with and without witnesses, and the ruling on investigative magic (*Zone of Truth*, *Speak with Dead*, *Detect Thoughts*, illusions, charm, *Modify Memory*).
> The six-stage clock below is canonical. WP01 adds **when it ticks** and **what moves it backwards**.

NPC investigations should operate independently of the players.

For serious crimes, create an investigation clock.

Example:

### Murder Investigation

1. Crime discovered
2. Witnesses interviewed
3. Evidence collected
4. Suspects identified
5. Surveillance / questioning
6. Arrest attempt or public accusation

Player actions may:
- accelerate;
- delay;
- redirect;
- expose;
- corrupt;
- obstruct

the investigation.

Failed attempts to conceal crimes can increase suspicion.

# 13. Faction Clocks

> **✅ Implemented by WP01 — see [[WP01 — Campaign State Model]] §6 *Faction Clocks*.**
> WP01 defines five canonical clocks with full stage text and advancement triggers: **Greybank Reaction**, **Laughing Hollow Intervention**, **Red-Tusk Strategic State**, **Daggerford Commercial Involvement**, **Illefarn Instability**.
> Two changes from the sketch below: clocks describe **behaviour**, not generic hostility; and the Red-Tusk clock **branches** after stage 3 into **War / Compact / Flight**.
> Ignored non-faction problems use the separate six-stage threat escalation in WP01 §12, not a faction clock.

Each major faction or threat receives a six-step clock.

Players may advance or reverse clocks.

Ignoring a problem usually advances it.

Example:

## Goblin Clock

1. Scouting
2. Raiding
3. Consolidating
4. Fortifying
5. Assault
6. War / migration

## Elven Clock

1. Observation
2. Warning
3. Interference
4. Sabotage
5. Blockade
6. Armed intervention

## Illefarn Instability

1. Dormant
2. Disturbed
3. Active
4. Dangerous
5. Breaching
6. Catastrophic release

The campaign's final state should partially emerge from these clocks.

# 14. Encounter Design Philosophy

Encounters should fall into several categories.

## Type A — Fair Tactical Combat

Conventional combat is appropriate.

Purpose:
- test normal class abilities;
- consume resources;
- establish competence.

---

## Type B — Expensive Combat

The party can win conventionally, but the cost is high.

Possible cost:
- spell slots;
- healing;
- consumables;
- NPC deaths;
- time;
- faction consequences.

The players are encouraged to find a better method.

---

## Type C — Puzzle Predator

The enemy appears overwhelming until its behavior or weakness is understood.

Examples:
- cannot cross running water;
- hunts sound;
- regenerates unless exposed to a specific environmental condition;
- follows rigid territorial behavior;
- is vulnerable while feeding;
- cannot perceive stationary creatures.

The weakness must be discoverable.

---

## Type D — Environmental Threat

Victory is achieved by manipulating terrain.

Examples:
- collapsing tunnels;
- flooding chambers;
- burning structures;
- moving machinery;
- activating magical wards.

---

## Type E — Social/Tactical Threat

Combat is possible but strategically disastrous.

Examples:
- village militia;
- elf patrol;
- goblin warband;
- merchant caravan guards.

---

## Type F — Apex Threat

The creature cannot reasonably be defeated when first encountered.

The encounter is about:
- survival;
- information;
- escape;
- diversion;
- sealing;
- future preparation.

Players may return later.

---

# 15. Enemy Behavior

Enemies should behave according to goals rather than suicidal combat logic.

They may:
- retreat;
- surrender;
- ambush;
- negotiate;
- call reinforcements;
- flee;
- take hostages;
- burn structures;
- steal resources;
- attack objectives instead of players.

Use morale checks for intelligent enemies.

Possible triggers:
- leader killed;
- half the group incapacitated;
- objective lost;
- overwhelming magic displayed;
- escape route opened.

Survivors remember encounters.

Escaped enemies may return later with:
- information;
- reinforcements;
- countermeasures.

---

# 16. Time Pressure

> **✅ Implemented by WP01 — see [[WP01 — Campaign State Model]] §10 *Time Model* (4-hour watches, time costs, watch transition) and §12 *Threat Escalation*.**
> Canonical: campaign time runs in **4-hour watches**, and world events progress on **stated deadlines** rather than because the party happened to act. WP01 §12.2 works the exact wolf/goblin/quarry example below all the way through.

Time must continue whether or not players act.

The party should never be able to resolve every problem.

Examples:

Three simultaneous rumors:

- wolves attacking livestock;
- goblins requesting a meeting;
- movement beneath the quarry.

Players investigate the quarry.

Meanwhile:
- wolves attack again;
- goblins interpret silence as rejection.

The world advances.

This creates meaningful prioritization.

---

# 17. Resting

> **✅ Implemented by WP01 — see [[WP01 — Campaign State Model]] §11 *Rest Procedure*.**
> Canonical: a long rest costs **2 watches**, a short rest roughly **1/4 watch**. WP01 §11.2 gives the six-step procedure for resolving what the world does while the party sleeps.

Long rests should remain possible but should have opportunity costs.

The game should avoid arbitrary “you cannot rest here” restrictions.

Instead:

A long rest consumes roughly eight hours.

During those eight hours:
- enemies move;
- fires spread;
- prisoners escape;
- faction clocks advance;
- wounded NPCs may die;
- caravans depart;
- weather changes.

Rest remains mechanically legitimate.

Time is the cost.

## Elven Ward

Upgrades should require:
- money;
- materials;
- NPC cooperation;
- time.

# 19. The Illefarn Site

> **✅ State layer implemented by WP01 — see [[WP01 — Campaign State Model]] §15 *Illefarn State Framework*:** instability, sectors, systems, the activation-consequence rule, and the breach rule.
> **Content layer belongs to WP06.** WP01 guarantees that any mechanism WP06 designs can be expressed as overworld state; WP06 must speak WP01's sector/system vocabulary rather than introduce new meters.
> The Stage I–IV progression below remains canonical. Per the DM's working note, **each session should be structured so the party *can* end it with some dungeon progress** — without predetermining that they will.

**[LORE]**
The campaign draws on ancient Illefarn history in the region.

**[CAMPAIGN]**
The specific Greybank facility is entirely original.

The site was an ancient border-defense and infrastructure installation.

Its systems may include:
- warning wards;
- magical barriers;
- transit routes;
- water control;
- defensive constructs;
- monster deterrents;
- observation devices.

The site should unlock progressively.

### Stage I — Outer Station
Level 3 content.

### Stage II — Workshops / Service Areas
Level 4 content.

### Stage III — Transit / Defense Network
Level 4–5 content.

### Stage IV — Central Ward
Level 5–6 content.

Reactivating systems should modify the overworld.

Example:

Activate Ward Stone:
- undead activity drops;
- elves notice;
- another sealed route opens;
- Illefarn instability changes.

Dungeon progression therefore alters the campaign rather than simply producing loot.

# 20. Loot Philosophy

Loot should create strategic choices.

Avoid excessive generic treasure.

Prioritize:
- limited consumables;
- strange magical tools;
- keys;
- maps;
- faction resources;
- infrastructure components;
- information.

Many magical items should have unusual utility rather than simple numeric bonuses.

Example:

**Illefarn Resonance Key**
- opens ancient ward doors;
- can disable one defensive construct;
- highly valuable to Daggerford collectors.

Players must choose whether to:
- keep it;
- sell it;
- give it to the elves;
- destroy it.

# 21. Village Familiarity

> **✅ Persistence layer implemented by WP01 — see [[WP01 — Campaign State Model]] §13 *NPC State*:** the compact NPC table, status, suspicion, NPC cards, and when a full sheet is warranted.
> **Roster content belongs to WP03**, which owns biographies; **placement and household structure belong to WP02**. WP01 fixes the minimum tracked state per recurring NPC, and the rule that dead NPCs stay dead and their services stay gone until a plausible replacement emerges (WP01 §5.11).

The village should contain approximately:

**20–30 named recurring NPCs**

Not all require full stat blocks.

Important NPCs receive:

- Name
- Occupation
- Personality
- Want
- Fear
- Resource
- Relationship
- Secret, if relevant

Deaths and relationship changes must persist.

# 22. Player Choice Philosophy

Do not create fake choices.

If players can choose:

A, B, or C

then those choices should cause meaningfully different outcomes.

Avoid:
- quantum ogres;
- relocating encounters simply because players chose another direction;
- forcing prepared scenes into unrelated locations;
- secretly guaranteeing the same outcome.

Unused material may remain unused.

That is acceptable.

---

# 23. Failure

Failure should usually change the game rather than stop it.

Examples:

Failed defense:
- building destroyed.

Failed negotiation:
- faction hostility increases.

Failed rescue:
- NPC dies.

Failed stealth:
- enemy reinforces.

Failed investigation:
- murderer escapes.

Some failures may cause character death.

Avoid requiring one successful skill check for campaign continuation.

---

# 24. Campaign End States

The campaign should allow multiple legitimate outcomes.

Examples:

### Greybank Fortified
The village survives and becomes stronger.

### Greybank Abandoned
The population evacuates.

### Goblin Alliance
Humans and goblins form an unstable cooperative settlement.

### Elven Protectorate
The elves impose control over the region.

### Merchant Boom
Greybank becomes wealthy but artifacts are stripped away.

### Illefarn Disaster
Ancient systems destabilize the region.

### Village Destruction
Greybank is destroyed.

### Party Outlawed
The adventurers become enemies of the settlement.

### Mixed Outcome
Most likely result.

There is no mandatory “good ending.”

# 25. DM Preparation Model

> **✅ Implemented by WP01 — see [[WP01 — Campaign State Model]] §18 *DM Update Procedure* (during play, after a major scene, end of session, before next session) and §17 *Randomness vs Determinism*.**
> Canonical operating principle: **the DM prepares what happens if the players do nothing.** Player choices then alter or replace those outcomes. WP01 §16 caps automatic consequence chains at **two links** unless a major event directly changes multiple systems.

Prepare situations rather than scripts.

Before each session determine:

1. What each faction wants.
2. What each faction will do if the players do nothing.
3. Which clocks advance.
4. Which resources changed.
5. Which NPCs changed attitude.
6. Which physical locations changed.
7. What consequences from the previous session now become visible.
8. What dangerous encounters currently exist.

Do not predetermine the player's route.

> **✅ Implemented by WP01 — see [[WP01 — Campaign State Model]] §18.3 *End of Session*, which expands this into nine ordered steps, and §19 *DM Dashboard Prototype*, which is the required information architecture for WP16.**

### Ask each player:

# 28. Tone

The desired tone is:

- dangerous;
- grounded;
- unsentimental;
- mysterious;
- consequence-driven;
- tactically rewarding.

Avoid:
- excessive exposition;
- endless lore dumps;
- invincible DMPCs;
- obvious quest markers;
- cartoonishly evil factions;
- guaranteed heroic outcomes.

Humor created by players is fine.

The world itself should remain internally serious.

---

# 29. Fairness Contract

The DM is allowed to be harsh.

The DM should not be deceptive about basic reality.

Players should understand:

- the world does not scale;
- retreat is valid;
- NPCs remember;
- crimes matter;
- resources are finite;
- characters can die;
- spare characters are recommended;
- some encounters are designed to be solved indirectly.

In exchange, the DM should:
- telegraph extreme danger;
- allow clever plans to work;
- respect player choices;
- avoid hidden forced outcomes;
- avoid retroactively changing facts to defeat player plans.

The campaign should feel ruthless but fair.

---

# 30. Target Experience

By the end of four sessions, players should be able to say things like:

> “We shouldn't have killed that goblin scout.”

> “If we'd protected the bridge, we could still buy potions.”

> “The creature wasn't impossible — we just fought it wrong.”

> “We caused this.”

> “Greybank looks completely different because of what we did.”

> “We absolutely should have run.”

Those reactions indicate that the campaign systems are working.

- Greybank is campaign-original.
- The campaign lasts four sessions, optionally five.
- Characters begin at level 3.
- Expected ending level is 5–6.
- Campaign is mechanics-first and story-light.
- The world is persistent.
- Resources do not magically replenish.
- Crimes are investigated.
- Murder can permanently remove services.

- The world does not scale to character level.
- Some encounters cannot reasonably be defeated conventionally.
- Dangerous encounters should provide discoverable warnings or weaknesses.

## 33.1 Canon added by WP01 ✅

The following are canonical as of [[WP01 — Campaign State Model]] and bind every later work package:

- Greybank tracks exactly four condition variables: **Food / Trade / Defense / Unity**, 0–6, starting **4 / 3 / 2 / 4**.
- Faction *relations* are replaced by **party reputation**, −3 to +3, tracked separately for **Greybank (0), Laughing Hollow (0), Red-Tusk (−1), Daggerford Interests (0)**. Individual NPC attitude remains independent.
- Five clocks are tracked: **Greybank Reaction, Laughing Hollow Intervention, Red-Tusk Strategic State, Daggerford Commercial Involvement, Illefarn Instability**. Clocks describe behaviour, not hostility. **Red-Tusk branches** after stage 3 into War / Compact / Flight.
- Non-faction problems use the six-stage threat ladder: **Sign → Incident → Escalation → Response → Collateral consequence → Regional outcome**, advancing only on defined triggers.
- Campaign time runs in **4-hour watches**. Long rest = **2 watches**. Short rest ≈ **1/4 watch**. Events fire on stated deadlines, not on party activity.
- Resources use a three-class model; only **critical discrete stock** is counted. Consumed, stolen or destroyed goods stay gone; replenishment needs a functioning service, sufficient Trade, and elapsed time.
- Pricing uses a **single worst applicable scarcity modifier**, then reputation adjustment. Modifiers do not stack.
- Infrastructure uses the state vocabulary **Improved / Intact / Damaged / Disabled / Destroyed**.
- Serious crime creates an investigation on a fixed six-stage clock with **Weak / Moderate / Strong** evidence. There is no courtroom subsystem.
- **Investigative magic is not nerfed** — it works by normal 5e rules; scarcity of access is the limiter.
- Automatic consequence chains stop after **two links** unless a major event directly changes several systems.
- Deterministic consequences stay deterministic. Dice are rolled only where uncertainty is meaningful.
- Illefarn is tracked as **Instability 0–6 + sector status + discovered systems + active systems + breaches**. Dungeon changes persist into the overworld.
- The DM prepares **what happens if the players do nothing**; player choices alter or replace those outcomes.
- **WP01 §19 fixes the DM dashboard's information architecture**, which WP16 must implement.

## 33.2 Canon added by WP02 ✅

The following are canonical as of [[WP02 — Greybank Village]] and bind every later work package:

- Greybank is **143 people in 34 households**, on the **north bank of the Marrow**, two days from Daggerford, at the only bridge for a day's travel. It is **500 yards end to end**.
- The village exists to cut **limestone**. Dressed stone is its principal export; this is why it has a bridge, a road, a wharf, and Daggerford's attention.
- There are **seven ways in**: bridge, haul road, north footpath, east fields, south-east riverside path, the shoal at low water, and the wharf by boat. **The village watches the first two.**
- The **earth ring is breached in four places** and provides no defensive benefit. Closing it is the campaign's first-tier defensive project.
- Anywhere inside the ring is **under 30 rounds** from anywhere else at a run. The quarry is **70 rounds** — help from the quarry never arrives during a fight in the village.
- **22 keyed locations, G1–G22.** Locations without a gameplay function are not keyed.
- **Five load-bearing service providers**, in order of consequence: **Maud Elleth** (medicine), **Ordga Brune** (metal), **Sember Dray** (imports), **Enna Fisk** (construction), **Gedd Plank** (the Daggerford run).
- **The shrine has no spellcaster.** Yorrin Fell is a lay-keeper who cannot cast. Divine magic must be brought, imported, or played by a PC. This preserves WP01's medicine scarcity and keeps investigative magic rare without nerfing it.
- **≈260 gp of liquid coin exists in the entire village.** Greybank cannot buy the party's loot; selling requires Daggerford, which advances the Daggerford clock.
- Critical stock is placed across **five locations**, and **half of it is not purchasable** — the shrine potion, the militia shield and ammunition, the chain shirt and the two horses are social transactions gated behind consent, reputation or theft.
- **Healer's kits replenish locally** at 1 per 3 days and are therefore Trade-independent. **Potions, armour and horses never replenish.**
- **6 charges of blasting powder** exist at the quarry — the only thing in Greybank that can drop a bridge pier or seal a passage at speed.
- The militia is **18 names, 11 prompt at Defense 2, ~20 minutes to muster.** Nine of the eighteen are quarry crew: **Greybank cannot mobilize and quarry at the same time.**
- **The bell does not reach** the quarry, the bunkhouse, the fold, the charcoal camp, or the outlying farmsteads — where 40 of the 143 are during a working day.
- Greybank is thatch around a stone core. **The store's slate roof is the Street's firebreak.** Fire spreads automatically only in the Cotts, the wagonyard, and the mill/granary pair.
- **Deep Night has 2–3 people awake.** The two blind spots are **the fold at night** and **the river at any time** — the Marrow is opaque and unwatched, and WP01 §7.4 concealment is adjudicated against WP02 §16.2 rather than general impressions.
- Village projects cost **coin, materials, labour, time and consent** — and consent is the expensive one. **No upgrade is a correct purchase**; each costs the village something it needs.
- **Quarry expansion raises Illefarn Instability +1** and is the village's only route to prosperity. The price is stated in advance and both halves of it are real.
- The **Reeve** is chosen and removable by the moot, which means a PC can hold the office — and inherit the labour levy, the moot chest, and the blame.
- **Reputation changes access, not inventory.** At Greybank Rep −2 the potion is still on the shelf; it is simply not for sale, and taking it is theft with a witness. Stock and attitude are tracked separately and must stay that way — no later package may make refused goods quietly disappear.
- **Greybank Rep −2 is quiet, not violent.** Services close, conversations stop, the taproom finds it has no rooms. Village mobilization under WP01 §9 is a **−3** event.
- WP02 adds one tracked structure to WP01 §14's list — **the taproom**, because Greybank has exactly one and its loss removes all lodging and makes addressing the village as a group harder. It uses the states **Intact / Closed**.
- WP02 uses three **specific functional states** beyond WP01's five-word vocabulary, under WP01 §22's allowance: **Watchtower — manned / unmanned at night**; **Shrine — Desecrated**; **Taproom — Intact / Closed**.
- **Fire is a local procedure, not a tracked system** — the WP01 §12 escalation ladder run at combat tempo, ending when the fire is out. This is the standing precedent for how later packages handle fast-moving physical hazards: **reuse the ladder at the appropriate tempo rather than inventing a subsystem.**
- **Granary destroyed → Food −2** is a *direct* effect, not a cascade, because the granary is the physical stock behind the variable. WP01 §16's two-link cascade limit is unaffected by it.

## 33.3 Canon added by WP03 ✅

The following are canonical as of [[WP03 — NPC Roster]] and bind every later work package:

- Greybank has **28 named recurring NPCs** and **115 deliberately unnamed villagers**. Promote an unnamed villager only when the players ask about them twice. Do not name someone to fill a gap in the roster.
- **Every recurring NPC has a "tonight" line** — what they do if the party never engages them. Tonight lines are not hooks, they **advance between sessions**, and they collide with each other. A tonight line unchanged by session 3 is a defect.
- Every recurring NPC carries a **voice handle** — one performable speech habit — alongside WP01 §13.4's want / fear / resource / attitude. **WP04's faction NPCs use this same format.** There is one NPC standard in this campaign, not two.
- **Starting attitudes are not uniform**: 4 Friendly, 19 Neutral, 5 Wary, 0 Hostile. Attitude moves **one step**, and only on something that happened to that NPC or theirs.
- **Lisbet Ash is the designated demonstration of attitude overriding reputation**, per WP01 §4.6's own worked example — *"the miller's daughter remains Friendly."*
- **Seven deaths make someone Hostile permanently.** Two are unrepairable — **Pell** and **Wat Dray**, both children. That they are children must be telegraphed before it matters.
- **Seven secrets exist and none connects to another.** Three change nothing mechanically. No secret sits behind a single check, and if the party finds none of them, nothing is lost.
- **Maud Elleth reading an injury is Moderate evidence** under WP01 §7.2. She cannot cast, but thirty years of treating wounds is real expertise, and lying to her about one is a genuine risk.
- **Five NPCs acquire suspicion disproportionately**: Nissa Crake (awake at night), Pell (everywhere), Old Tarn (watches the Green, unreliably), Maud Elleth (reads injuries), Denk Ottersby (the crossing book).
- **No villager will enter the Illefarn site**, for any inducement. Torchbearers and hirelings must be imported.
- **The militia never crosses the boundary stones.** Berrick Hone and Nissa Crake are the only two villagers who can shoot, and Berrick may break if left alone — that uncertainty is deliberate and should stay uncertain.
- Greybank's internal conflict **requires no faction machinery**. Three tensions run from day one: **Hone vs Durrow** (nine of the eighteen militia are quarry crew), **Orrick vs Durrow** (the levy takes hands six weeks before harvest), and **the village vs the bunkhouse** (six Daggerford men outside the ring who hear no bell).
- **A dead PC's replacement may be drawn from the roster** — including the relative of someone the party failed to save. WP02's empty cott H22 exists for this.

The following are canonical as of [[WP04 — Factions]] and bind every later work package:

The isolationists have no leverage at Unity ≥3 and become the loudest voice at Unity ≤2, because theirs is the only position requiring no cooperation.

## 33.5 Canon added by WP05 ✅

The following are canonical as of [[WP05 — Regional Map]] and bind every later work package:

- **Four crossings of the Marrow:** the bridge; the shoal (late summer only); **Anvil Gorge** (foot, single file, no cargo); and **Bythe Ford** — the cart detour, **+4 watches round trip, impassable in spate**. Plus the skiff, four at a time. This answers WP01 §14.1.
- **Spate closes the ford and the shoal together.** A destroyed bridge in spate leaves Greybank with **no cart route at all** for 1–2 days, strands Ib Sallow on the far bank, halves the smithy and stalls every timber project. **Spate is a DM tool, telegraphed a day ahead through Corvin Ash — never rolled.**

Plotted, they form an **arc open to the north-west**, with the two doors — the quarry aperture and the Old Delving — at its focus and its end. **The pattern is discoverable by walking, looking, and asking Ordga Brune. WP06 owns what it means; the waystones are never lootable.**
- **The Old Delving is where the Red-Tusk fled from**, four watches north-west, **piled shut from the outside**. It is **Stage 3 — Escalation** and the only Stage 3 on the map at campaign start.

- **Fenrow Holding is three households and no services** — not a second Greybank, and it must never become one.
- **Nobody in Greybank has been past Hagsfoot Moor.** Four zones have never been visited by anyone now living in the village. Greybank knows six zones; the Red-Tusk know nine; the wardens know all eighteen.

## 33.6 Canon added by WP06 ✅

The following are canonical as of [[WP06 — Illefarn Dungeon]] and bind every later work package:

- The site is **the Marrowward — a frontier ward line, not a vault.** WP05's six waystones **are** its anchors, the arc **is** the perimeter, and the two gates sit at its focus and its end. **Three of the six spans have been dead for an age**, and they face north-west.
- **Nothing went catastrophically wrong. The site has been failing by attrition for two thousand years**, and Instability 1 is what that looks like when nobody has poked it.
- **The apex threat is the site's own defensive construct** — the Sentinel. Its instruction survived; its map did not. It is **not intelligent, not evil, not negotiable, and not killable at levels 3–6.** It has **four solutions, none of them a fight.**
- **This is what emptied the Old Delving.** The Red-Tusk were inside the perimeter and were classified as an incursion. Their account is accurate and uncomprehending.
- **The Red-Tusk have been holding the Kettlerock Gate shut without knowing it.** On the Flight branch the gate breaches permanently and **the Sentinel walks out** — Stonecrop Rise, then Wend's Barrow, then Greybank, one waystone per night.
- **Every stage is exitable at the level it is entered, by at least two routes, none of them a check.** **Every barrier has at least two ways through it.** No stage is gated behind one roll.
- **Stage I is deliberately generous.** Nothing in it can kill a careful level 3 party, and the Watch-Glass shows the entire ward line — three anchors dark, facing north-west — free, in session 1.
- **The Stage IV barrier is a challenge that accepts a name and does not punish a wrong answer.** Every route to the deepest part of the site runs through a faction relationship, an hour of patient reading, or a climb down a broken fence — **none of them runs through a fight.**
- **Every system has a written regional consequence** (WP06 §14), and **every system except the Watch-Glass moves at least one faction clock. There is no quiet way to use this place.**
- **Two systems lower Instability by repairing rather than exploiting.** The site rewards repair.
- **The transit route puts Greybank and the Old Delving half a watch apart underground**, against four on the surface — and it is the single act most likely to push the Laughing Hollow to armed confrontation.
- **The water control governs the Marrow itself** — and therefore the mill, the shoal and Bythe Ford. Draining the flooded gallery stops Greybank's mill.
- **The Laughing Hollow is right about every fact and wrong about one possibility** — that the site can only be sealed, never repaired. They never exaggerated. **Nobody in the campaign holds the whole picture, including them.**
- **Nothing at the site advances on a timer.** Every step up the Instability clock has a name and a person who took it.
- **The good ending is expensive, cooperative and available**: re-anchoring the Heart needs the Anchor stabilised, two more living spans including the elves' own, the Sentinel corrected, and an uninterrupted watch. **Destroying a second waystone puts it permanently out of reach.**
- **Enna Fisk the wright is unrecoverable.** Her death raises every build and repair time in Greybank by **50%, permanently**, and **no replacement exists — not within four sessions, not through any faction clock, not ever.** No later package may introduce a joiner to soften this. She is the roster's proof that §2.3's promise about permanent NPC death is literal. The obligation this creates is **telegraphing her centrality, not providing a substitute** (§29).


## 33.7 Canon added by WP07 ✅

The following are canonical as of [[WP07 — Hardcore Encounter Library]] and bind every later work package. **WP07 §1.3's thirteen design decisions were reviewed and confirmed by the DM on completion of the package; they are canon, not open questions.**

- **Every encounter carries eight fields** (§14's list, made mandatory), and **every Type C, D and F encounter has at least one weakness discoverable by scouting, asking an NPC, or watching** — never behind a single check.
- **No encounter may have combat as its only solution, and none may have exactly one non-combat solution.**
- **There is no CR budgeting in this campaign.** Stated difficulty describes what a fight costs; it is never a licence to move, scale or soften an encounter. **Never adjust an encounter because the party arrived early.**
- **Morale is WP01 §17.1's d6**, rolled once per group per trigger. **The Sentinel, the wardens, the swarms, and the Moorgaunt while feeding never check.**
- **Survivors remember, specifically.** Every group that escapes does a named thing next session, written down at WP01 §18.3's update. **The Sentinel is the one thing that escapes being escaped and does nothing about it.**
- **Defense sets numbers, positions and preparation; only WP02 §15's militia training project buys competence.** WP02 §12.3's turnout and muster figures are unchanged.
- **The Red-Tusk are 1 leader, 3 scouts, 14 spear and 4 bow, and there will never be more.** The band never commits all twenty-two; **eight is the most ever seen in one place before W6.** Ghesh withdraws at six down by decision, not by morale roll. **The thirty-nine are never statted.**
- **The party never fights nine wardens.** Every warden breaks contact after two full rounds regardless of how the fight is going. **Every warden encounter is an interdiction, a recovery, or a warning.**
- **The Sentinel has no hit points.** Damage below 25 from a single source leaves no mark. Three postures — Warning, Removal, Suppression — escalating and never reversing within an encounter, with **one round of grace at thirty feet**. It does not learn, remember, or pursue past the perimeter.
- **What comes through the Broken Quarter is one starving upland predator — the Moorgaunt — and it is there because sixty-one goblins ate Hagsfoot Moor bare.** One shortage, two consequences, no mastermind.
- **The Moorgaunt caches and will not hunt while a cache holds meat**, is **vulnerable while feeding**, and **will not cross the Sentinel's groove in S10** — which is why the Marrowward is not already full of moor animals. **Removing the Sentinel opens the site to what the Sentinel was keeping out.**
- **Killing the Moorgaunt does not close the hole in the fence.** Only System 1 or System 7 does.
- **Two things take livestock at the fold, and telling them apart is the encounter.** Misreading it costs the Compact branch; the evidence to read it correctly is physical, on the ground, and on Bessa Ruddock's slate.
- **Killing the Listener opens the water channels to the Marrow**: Z6 Reedwater advances one stage within a tenday. That is the answer WP06 §11.1 deferred.
- **Stage I contains no creature at all.** Its dangers are a ramp, a shutter that breaks permanently, and an anchor that answers if forced.
- **Four hazards need no creature**: Anvil Gorge, Bythe Ford in spate, the Sluice Hall, and fire in Greybank. All four run WP01 §12's ladder at the appropriate tempo, per WP02's standing precedent — **no later package may invent a hazard subsystem.**
- **Draining S8 → mill Disabled → Food −1 is the canonical two-link cascade example**, and Unity −1 alongside it is a direct WP01 §3.4 effect, not a third link.
- **No encounter may require a specific class ability.** Nothing in this campaign is gated behind arcane magic, divine magic or thieves’ tools. Encounters are written for **five PCs of unknown mix**; adjust by one enemy per PC above or below five, and never adjust hazards or the Sentinel.
- **Forcing the S3 Ward Anchor deals 2d6 force damage to every creature in the room** and is **Instability +1**. The recess warms under the hand first — the warning is tactile and precedes the blow.
- **Ordinary animals are not statted anywhere in this campaign.** Wolves, bears and boars exist and a DM may use them freely; only creatures carrying a consequence get an entry. **A campaign document that stats a wolf has wasted a page.**
- **Stat blocks are printed in full, in the document that owns them.** No page references and nothing to look up mid-fight. Later packages adding a creature print it whole or cite WP07’s.

## 34.4 Standing requirements

Every chunk honours these, and **G8 verifies them**.

| Requirement | Rule |
|---|---|
| **Offline** | No CDN, no remote font, no `fetch`. Must work from `file://` with no network |
| **No build step** | Vanilla HTML, CSS, JS. Shared files loaded by `<script src>` and `<link rel=stylesheet>` |
| **State portability** | The DM runs this **across multiple machines**. `localStorage` is convenience only; **export and import of a single JSON file is the real mechanism** (§34.5) |
| **Placeholders** | Anything not yet written renders as a visible marked block naming its owning package. **Never invent content to fill a gap** |
| **Images** | Referenced by path into `assets/img/`, with a styled fallback when the file is absent. **Nothing breaks because an image has not been generated yet** |
| **Canon fidelity** | Every number, name and state change matches its source. Cite the document and section |
| **Linking** | Entity links auto-generated, **first mention per section only**, hover preview, click to navigate, browser Back preserved |

## 34.5 State model

**One JSON object, one file, versioned.** Exported as `broken-march-state.json`, imported by file picker, with a clipboard fallback for machines where file dialogs are awkward.

**What is tracked:** the WP01 §19 dashboard (four variables, four reputations, five clocks, infrastructure, critical stock, Illefarn sectors and systems, breaches, zone threat stages) · session progress (current day and watch, rumour cards dealt, deadlines fired) · free-text DM notes per section · UI preferences.

**What is never tracked:** anything that would make the guide the source of truth. **The vault documents remain canonical; the guide is a viewer with a scratchpad.**

## 34.6 Image pipeline

Images are generated separately from [[Session 1 Prep — Visual Assets (WP15)]] and will arrive **after** the guide is built.

1. Every chunk that needs an image references a **fixed filename** in `assets/img/`.
2. Until the file exists, the page shows a labelled placeholder carrying the filename and the prompt it came from.
3. **G8 produces `assets/img/README.md`** — the complete manifest of expected filenames, what each depicts, and which § of the Visual Assets document generated it.
4. The DM drops finished images into `assets/img/` using those exact names. **No code changes, no re-generation.**

## 34.7 What the guide must never do

- Become a second source of truth. **On any conflict, the vault documents win.**
- Invent an NPC, price, distance, monster, rule or outcome to fill a gap.
- State a clock value or a mechanic inside read-aloud text.
- Require a network connection, a server, or a build step.
